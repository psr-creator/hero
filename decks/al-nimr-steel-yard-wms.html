02Why WMS

Start with one question. It exposes the whole chain.

The opening discovery question
“When a customer asks for the mill test cert on a delivery from six months ago — how long does that take?”

It is specific, non-threatening, and almost always exposes the entire manual chain sitting behind it: the delivery note, the invoice file, the purchase file, the supplier's email, and finally a scanned PDF somebody has to find.

Operational pain — the eight that show up in every steel yard

Inventory identity

Critical

Steel doesn't scan like cartons. Heat numbers, mill test certs and grade/spec have to stay attached to each piece. Manual tracking means MTC hunting at delivery.

Exposure — a lost cert can reject a shipment on a certified project.

Dual UOM: pieces vs weight

Critical

Sold and costed by MT, stored and moved by piece or bundle. Hold only one unit and theoretical weight drifts from weighbridge actual.

Exposure — gross margin becomes fictional, invoice by invoice.

Remnants and cut pieces

High

Cutting and shearing create offcuts that get logged badly or vanish. The section is reordered anyway because nobody can see the remnant.

Exposure — cash sitting in the yard, bought twice.

Yard location accuracy

High

Open stockyards, crane and forklift movement, no bin structure. Staff know where things are by memory.

Exposure — one resignation and picking time doubles.

Stock aging & price exposure

High

Steel is a commodity with volatile input pricing. Slow-moving tonnage bought at an old price sits quietly against current market.

Exposure — GP dies silently without aging by grade and section.

Order promising

Critical

Sales quotes from a stock figure that is a day old, then discovers the section was already allocated to another job.

Exposure — missed dates on contracts carrying LD clauses.

Reservation vs allocation

Critical

Project customers block stock verbally. Without a system reservation with an owner and an expiry, the same batch gets sold twice.

Exposure — double-selling a heat you cannot substitute.

Physical counts

High

Wall-to-wall counts on heavy sections shut the yard down. Cycle counting is near-impossible without location and weight logic.

Exposure — lost trading days, once or twice a year.

Technological pain — why WMS projects fail, and what we do about it

Most failed WMS implementations are not software failures. They are data, process, integration and adoption failures that the software then gets blamed for. These are the eight we design against from day one.

Failure mode What it looks like on site Our control
Failure 01Dirty master data Duplicate SKUs, missing nominal weights, no grade or section standard, no location register. The system inherits the mess and confidence collapses in week one. Data cleanse is a gated workstream with its own sign-off — item master, UOM conversions, grades, heats and yard locations must pass before configuration starts.
Failure 02Paving the cowpath The current manual process is configured into software, so the same rework, the same double handling, the same verbal reservations — now with more clicks. Process redesign in the Define stage. We map as-is, agree to-be, and only then configure. Anything we automate has to survive that question first.
Failure 03Integration underestimated WMS and ERP disagree on stock. Sales trusts one screen, finance trusts the other, and reconciliation becomes a monthly job. Insight's Universal Data Interchange Hub — EDI, XML or flat file — with the interface contract signed at design and volume-tested before pilot. Proven against SAP, Dynamics AX/NAV/BC, Oracle EBS, Orion, Scala.
Failure 04Big-bang go-live Every zone, every SKU class, every process on one date. One defect stops trading, and the business reverts to spreadsheets to keep shipping. Phased rollout. One yard zone and one product class go live first as a pilot, with the manual fallback still available, then we scale by zone.
Failure 05Infrastructure ignored RF coverage dies at the far stack. Labels fade in the sun or peel in the humidity. Handhelds break the first time they're dropped near a crane. Site survey and coverage design before go-live, rugged and vehicle-mounted terminals, weather-durable tags, and an RFID-versus-barcode decision per zone — RFID counts roughly 100× faster, but not everywhere earns it.
Failure 06No process ownership No named owner for stock accuracy or cert attachment. When it gets busy, the yard reverts to memory and the data quietly rots. Named process owners per function, floor-level training in the operators' language, and a steering committee that reviews accuracy as a KPI, not an IT ticket.
Failure 07Scope creep Every stakeholder adds a report. The go-live date moves twice, the budget moves once, and momentum is gone before anyone sees value. PMI change control against a signed scope baseline. New asks are logged, costed and scheduled into a later phase — not absorbed silently.
Failure 08Success never defined No baseline was measured before go-live, so nobody can prove the system worked. The project is judged on anecdote. Baseline the metrics in section 04 during Discovery, instrument them in the system, and review them at 30, 60 and 90 days post go-live.
03How

Insight SCM Suite, configured for a stockyard — not a carton warehouse.

Insight is a set of tightly integrated, highly configurable supply chain modules covering Open Yard, Warehousing, Distribution, Transportation and Freight on one track-and-trace platform. It sits over your yard and talks to your ERP; it does not replace it.

Pain → mechanism

01
Inventory identityHeat no., MTC, grade, spec
Heat number and mill certificate attach to the piece or bundle at goods receipt and travel with it through cutting, transfer and despatch. Retrieval is a lookup from the delivery note — DN → heat → MTC.
02
Dual UOMPCS and MT on one line
Both units live on the same stock record with a per-item conversion. Weighbridge actual is captured against theoretical, variance is booked to a tolerance you set, and costing uses the weight you actually shipped.
03
RemnantsCut, shear, offcut
A cut is a transaction. The parent is consumed, the child remnant is created with inherited heat and cert lineage, given its own location and aging clock, and surfaced to sales before a fresh section is bought.
04
Yard locationOpen-yard, no racking
The yard is modelled as row / bay / stack rather than bin. RF-directed putaway and picking replace memory, so a new operator finds a section as fast as a ten-year man.
05
Aging & price exposureBy grade and section
Aging buckets by grade, section and heat, held against landed cost per batch. Slow-moving tonnage is flagged while you can still move it, not at year-end.
06
Order promisingReal-time ATP
Sales quotes against on-hand − allocated − reserved, live, on the same screen as the quote. No day-old stock sheet, no discovering the allocation after the promise.
07
Reservation vs allocationProject vs general stock
Project stock is reserved with an owner, a reference and an expiry; general distribution stock stays open. Hard allocation on order confirmation. The same heat cannot be sold twice.
08
CountingCycle, not wall-to-wall
Count by zone, grade or aging bucket on a rolling schedule, with weight tolerance logic for heavy sections. The yard keeps trading while it counts.

Around the core

Capture
Handheld and vehicle-mounted RF terminals with scanners for receipt, put-away, picking, transfer and despatch. Barcode where it earns its place, RFID where speed of count justifies the recurring tag cost.
Integrate
Universal Data Interchange Hub — purchase orders, ASNs and sales orders in; receipts and shipments out. EDI, XML and flat file, integrated at logic and database level rather than by file drop.
Report
Native dashboards, plus a data structure any BI tool can read. Power BI, or whatever you already run — stock by grade, aging by heat, weight variance, picking productivity.
